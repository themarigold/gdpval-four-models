**Bottom line**

1. **Board authority and the Agreement.** Under DGCL § 141(a), the board manages Avalon except as the statute or charter provides, and a stockholder agreement is neither. Under pre-amendment law, as applied in *Moelis*, Marcus's blanket pre-approval right and his officer-appointment right are likely unenforceable. If new § 122(18) governs, the Agreement is probably enforceable as to defined actions. Three problems remain: (a) the Agreement was signed in January 2024, before SB 313's August 1, 2024 effective date, so its applicability must be confirmed; (b) the charter's "exclusively managed" language gives a textual argument that the charter forecloses stockholder restrictions; and (c) a veto over "any corporate transaction" is broader than a defined restriction.

2. **Board deference.** The board cannot abdicate its judgment to a stockholder, and the business judgment rule protects decisions the board makes, not outcomes dictated by a consent right. Declining the deal to "appease" Marcus is the weakest point. All directors are his appointees, which invites independence and loyalty claims. With no § 102(b)(7) exculpation (per the assumptions), care-based exposure also remains.

3. **Marcus's duties.** His power to appoint every director and officer and to veto every transaction makes controller status likely to be pleaded successfully, even with a minority stake. The board's own deference is evidence of that control. A veto driven by personal spite is a credible disloyalty theory, but a contestable one: the harm falls pro rata on all stockholders, and I found no Delaware authority squarely treating non-financial animus as a "non-ratable benefit."
