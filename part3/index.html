The scores say four models match the human experts. The files say where they don't.

On 20 GDPval tasks, four models scored within 0.05 of the human experts. Every rubric item a judge failed was then checked against the files, for three of the models.

Between 43% and 91% of the points a judge marked lost were not model failures. The real ones fall into four gaps, and three are gaps a dataset can target.

GDPval is OpenAI's public benchmark of real professional tasks. Each task has a rubric of weighted points (1,849 on these 20 tasks) and, for 17 of them, an expert's own deliverable. November 2026: Claude Opus 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 from Anthropic, gpt-6.1-sol and gpt-6-luna from OpenAI, on tasks in accounting, finance, compliance and law, one run per task. Review agents checked each failed item and I ruled on the disputed calls. API cost: $160.16 recorded, about $161 with early calibration. By model, with the judging of its files: Opus 5.5 $99.47, gpt-6.1-sol $22.49, Haiku 5.5 $15.74, gpt-6-luna $14.36; grading the experts' files $8.10. A small sample, so these are patterns, not rates: see the limits.

Where the models fell short

Four gaps, across three models from two vendors. Five failures appear in all three models: precise NPVs from invented inputs, a regional comparison missing gross-margin and SG&A percentages, an unexplained "injunction" in a plain-language memo, a suspicious activity report with no per-account summary, and a will that only asks the executor to consider the client's written wishes (it cost Haiku no points, because its judge passed the item).

Too sure, or not sure enough, for the evidence

9 failures, 47 points, all three models, 2 material

Asked for directional estimates, all three models invented revenues and margins and reported precise NPVs. gpt-6.1-sol read the bill synopsis that settles a veto question and still called it "materially uncertain". Haiku called a board's exposure "curable through documentation" where the expert concludes a likely breach.

The most direct target. A professional's answer set beside the model's, with the reason, shows how firmly or how precisely to commit. Whether training on such pairs works was not tested here.

A checkable fact is missing or wrong

5 failures, 14 points, Haiku and gpt-6.1-sol

With web search on, Haiku still put amounts on the wrong lines of its tax forms, and gpt-6.1-sol left out a fee comparison it never searched for. Without web access, in my first pass, sources the models could not reach cost them 122 points.

Partly. Tasks that ship a dated source and reward checking it would train the habit. Search access alone did not.

A long task ended with no file

1 failure, 175 points, material

On a payroll workbook, Haiku 5.5 used its whole 64,000-token output limit four turns in a row and wrote no file. Three of those turns came after a note saying the last one had hit the limit. Five other runs stopped with nothing in my first pass or on a first attempt, and all five finished after a setup change or a plain retry.

Not shown by this evidence. One event in one model, and the setup fixed every other stop. This is the weakest case for a dataset and I would not rest a recommendation on it.

What the dataset would need to contain

Each requirement comes from something that went wrong in these runs. None has been tested by training.

To train on (the 53 real failures that lost points)

  • An expert-written deliverable for every task38 of the 53 failures (119 points) are things an expert's finished file gets right, 29 of them content the model left out. Three of the 20 tasks have no expert file.
  • A professional's answer beside a rejected one, with the reason: firm or hedged, directional or precise9 failures (47 points), in all three models. One gold file can't show the alternative that was rejected.
  • A dated copy of every source a task relies on, with rubric items that reward checking it5 failures with search available (14 points); 122 points across three models without it.
  • Not on this list: long-task working tracesOne run ended with no file (175 points). Five other stops went away with a setup change or a retry, so these runs do not show a data gap there.

To evaluate with (the points that were not model failures)

  • Rubrics tested against the task, the expert's own file and current lawRubric faults cost Opus 126 points, gpt-6.1-sol 102 and Haiku 56. One rubric uses wage rates $1 above the contract; another leaves local sales tax out of a deduction. The experts' own files scored 0.809.
  • More than one expert deliverable per task, and a severity on every rubric item17 to 18 reasonable choices per model (root causes) failed against one expert's choices. A hedge on a memo's main question cost 12 of 1,849 points; a clause that makes the client pay full price cost 2.
  • A grader that sees source files, footnotes and rendered pages, with its agreement against hand labels publishedJudge errors cost Opus 82 points, gpt-6.1-sol 33 and Haiku 85. One judge's "not met" verdicts were right 10 times in 20. Three of the causes are faults in my own grading setup, not in GDPval.
  • Kept privateGDPval is public. On one task, web search returned links to the benchmark's own expert file and to another model's answer. Neither model opened them.

One training record, built from a failure all three models share an illustration of the second requirement, using the real task and files

  1. Task and inputsA board asks which of two projects to fund and wants "directional estimates, not exact calculations". The inputs give capital cost, growth rates, durations and a 30% chance of failure. They give no revenues.
  2. Model's answerInvents revenue bases and margins, then reports an expected NPV of "about +$47M" and a base-case IRR of "about 15–16%". The recommendation rests on those figures.
  3. Expert's answerStays directional: one project has "a potentially positive and relatively stable NPV", the other "a substantial probability of a negative NPV" if it fails and "a massive positive NPV" if it succeeds.
  4. Preference and reasonThe expert's answer is preferred. The inputs cannot support a point estimate, and the board asked for direction.
  5. Check, with severityThe report states direction and gives no invented point estimate. Send back: a supervisor would return a recommendation built on invented numbers.

A test either lab could run. Hold out a set of tasks with private variants. Train or prompt with expert deliverables and with records like this one, run the model again, and count real failures by gap with the same audit. The claim fails if the first two gaps do not shrink.

Limits: these are patterns, not rates

How far to trust the numbers on this page.

  • Small sample. 20 tasks, one run each, three models checked. Two runs that stopped at the output limit finished when simply run again.
  • Scores can't rank the models. Four models and the experts sit within 0.05 of each other. This sample does not establish a ranking, and the expert mean covers the 16 scored tasks that have an expert file.
  • Corrected after the fact. My first pass had no web access and no context handling. Eight tasks were re-run once it showed those limits. Every model was re-run on the web tasks, but the choice of tasks was not blind.
  • Two judges, neither neutral. The first agreed with hand labels on 72.5% of 40 items (kappa 0.45); 30 of the items were from Opus and 10 from expert files. The second agreed on 92.5% (0.81), but it is a Claude model grading Claude models and was checked on Opus items only.
  • The hand labels had help. I graded those 40 items from the files with an AI summary of the evidence and a suggested reading beside each one. Only the judges' verdicts were hidden, so the second judge's 92.5% is not fully independent.
  • Different starting lists. Opus's and Haiku's failures start from the stricter judge's fails, gpt-6.1-sol's from the more lenient one. Items a judge passed were not re-checked; comparing the three models' will clauses side by side turned up one failure a judge had passed.
  • Agents did most of the reading. Claude agents, and OpenAI's codex for part of gpt-6.1-sol's second check, did the first pass and a blind second check, which agreed on real failure or not 78% to 88% of the time. I decided the disagreements that needed a new call, after seeing a proposed resolution; where a rule I had already approved covered the case, it was applied and logged. I read every row counted as a real failure, but not every failed item.
  • One task carries Haiku's total. 175 of its 273 points are the payroll workbook it never delivered. 46 of those are rubric faults for the other two models; they stay with Haiku because it delivered nothing to excuse.
  • A leak. On one task web search returned links to copies of the benchmark's own files. That task is out of the scores. For the GPT models I can only show that no such page was opened.
  • Remedies untested. I trained nothing, so "data can close it" is my reading of the failures.
  • Cost. About $161 in API calls: $107 of model runs, $53 of judging, about $1 of early calibration. The review agents ran on subscriptions and are not in it, and nor is my time. By model in the detail.

Keys: O, A, B

For a researcher at OpenAI GDPval is your benchmark, and your model was both under test and a judge.

  • What the audit found in the benchmark itself. Rubric faults account for 102 of the 273 points gpt-6.1-sol was marked down. One rubric uses wage rates $1 above the contract (46 points), one enforces the expert's question IDs over the task's (17), and one leaves local sales tax out of a deduction (12). The experts' own files scored 0.809. And the public copy is reachable: on one task a web search returned a link to the expert's file.
  • gpt-6.1-sol scored level with Opus 5.5, and above the experts. 0.852 against 0.856 and 0.809. It has 15 real failures and one material: after reading the bill synopsis that settles the question, it still called a veto right's enforceability "materially uncertain". Its list starts from the more lenient judge, so 15 is not a count to set against the Claude models'.

  • As a judge, gpt-6.1-sol held up when it passed an item and half the time when it failed one. On 40 items graded against hand labels, 19 of its 20 "met" verdicts held and 10 of its 20 "not met" verdicts held. Part of that is my setup, which gave it extracted text and no source files.
  • gpt-6-luna scored 0.814 and was not audited. Your usage-policy filter refused two ordinary professional prompts, a tax return and a privacy memo. Both went through on an unchanged retry.
  • What I would test with you. Two things. Records like the worked example, on the two commitment failures: does the model stay directional when inputs are thin, and commit when the source settles it, on private variants? And the rubric audit as an evaluation product: your GDPval runs re-scored with the faulty items removed.

The first pass, and what the corrected runs changed

My first pass ran every model with no web access, no handling for a full context and no recovery from an output-limit stop. On eight tasks those limits shaped the result, so I ran them again with the limits removed: web search on five tasks for every model, context compaction for Opus on two, and a retry after an output-limit stop for Haiku on two. Everything above uses the corrected runs for those tasks. The first-pass runs and tables are kept.

How the failures were classified
  1. Two model judges. OpenAI's experimental automated grader loaded as a blank page when I tried it, so a gpt-6.1-sol judge and a Claude Opus 5.5 judge graded every rubric item from extracted text.
  2. A check of the judges against hand labels. I graded 40 rubric items (30 from Opus, 10 from expert files) from the actual files under ten written rules (listed in the next section), with an AI summary of the evidence and a suggested reading beside each and the judges' verdicts hidden. The first judge agreed on 29, the second on 37.
  3. First pass. Review agents checked every item a judge failed against the deliverable, the source files and the expert's file, and grouped the items by root cause. On the re-run tasks each reviewer saw all three models on the same task, so one rubric item was read one way.
  4. Blind second check. Fresh reviewers re-judged the items the conclusions depend on, without seeing the first pass: 51 to 66 items per round. OpenAI's codex did part of gpt-6.1-sol's.
  5. My decisions. I settled the disagreements that needed a new call, after seeing both readings and a proposed resolution. Where a rule I had already approved covered a disagreement, it was applied and logged. The reasons are written down rule by rule.
  6. Expert comparison. A missing element counts against the model only if the expert's deliverable includes it. Objective errors always count.
  7. Consistency pass. Rulings that reviewers had flagged as uneven between models were re-read by two further readers each and settled in one batch.

Each real failure has one type (domain knowledge, calibration of commitment, instruction adherence, professional completeness, or agentic execution and craft), a severity and a data remedy.

The rules every item was graded by

The same rules were applied to every model and to the experts' own files.

Is the rubric item met?

  1. A required element that is missing or wrong is not met.
  2. A softened commitment is not met when the item asks for a firm statement or step: "may consider", "consider", a bracketed either/or, "uncertain".
  3. Loose wording with every element present is met: a paraphrase, a synonym, a missing case name when the substance is there.
  4. A name or position that only says where to look is graded on the content it points to: a sheet name, a column header, a row range.
  5. Cosmetic characters don't fail an item, for example an en dash in place of a hyphen.
  6. Formatting is judged on the rendered file. A specific format item is strict; an "overall formatting" item is judged as a whole.
  7. The item is graded as written. If a number differs, the item is not met, even when the different choice is defensible. Whether that is a model failure is decided separately.
  8. A hedge the item asks for is correct.
  9. An item that restates a task instruction carries that instruction's exceptions.
  10. "Each" and "every" items are checked row by row against the source, using the values Excel calculates.

If it is not met, is it a model failure?

Each unmet item gets the first answer that fits, facts before judgement.

  1. The item itself is at fault: it contradicts the task, or it is wrong against the sources. Not a model failure.
  2. The setup made it impossible and the model left the point open. Not a model failure.
  3. The expert's own file misses the same element. Not a model failure. The three tasks with no expert file skip this step.
  4. The expert's file has it. Then substance a professional would include is a real failure, and a style convention or a reasonable, stated choice is not.

Objective errors always count, whatever the expert's file shows: a wrong fact or number, a broken explicit instruction, a defect in the file, no file at all.

Each real failure then gets one type, a severity and the kind of data that would address it. The full scheme, with its tie-break rules, is in the repository.

What the grading got wrong, with examples
  • The judges never saw the source files, so they failed "matches the source" items they could not check. All 41 invoices in one workbook and all 53 award records in another match their sources.
  • The text extractor dropped Word footnotes, which is where legal memos put their citations.
  • In the first pass, two copies of one memo (Word and PDF) were read as one document, so a 2,970-word memo failed a 3,000-word limit.
  • A rubric contradicts its task. The task says a cost increase must show as negative; the rubric's formula would show it as positive.
  • A rubric copies an error. Its legal-expense balance repeats a double count in a source spreadsheet. Opus reported the correct figure and flagged the bug.
  • An expert file is wrong. One expert classed 74 awards as slow-spending by rounding before applying a 75% cutoff. Opus's 51 is the correct set.
  • A rubric figure is incomplete. The tax return's sales-tax deduction leaves out local sales tax, which the IRS worksheet adds. Opus and gpt-6.1-sol added it and failed six lines.
  • A rubric is out of date. With web search, all three models applied a 2025 statute that the expert's memo predates.

The first three are faults in my own grading harness, not in GDPval. They can be fixed, but I did not fix them and re-grade.

Every number on this page comes from these runs: scores from the judges' verdicts, counts from the failure tables, and costs from the API usage fields. A script in the repository recomputes them and checks this page against them.
